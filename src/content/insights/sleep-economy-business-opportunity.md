The sleep economy is attractive because the customer problem is real. People are tired, stressed, time-poor, and increasingly willing to pay for relief. That is the start of the thinking, not the conclusion.

The question for an owner is not whether the category is growing. It is whether there is a specific customer, a repeatable reason to choose you, and an operating model that can make money without creating a new full-time job for the founder.

Start with four tests.

## 1. Is the problem urgent enough to pay for?

Interest is cheap. A business needs a customer who will make time, change behaviour, or allocate budget because the problem is painful enough now.

## 2. Can you name the narrow first customer?

"People who want better sleep" is a market category. It is not a first customer. A useful opening is narrower: shift workers, new parents, stressed executives, ageing consumers, or a defined employer group. The narrower definition makes the offer, channels, and proof easier to test.

## 3. Where does trust come from?

Sleep touches health, routine, devices, supplements, coaching, and data. Be careful not to imply clinical outcomes you cannot support. Decide whether your advantage is education, service, community, distribution, design, technology, or a trusted professional partner.

## 4. Can the delivery work without founder heroics?

Map the customer journey before building the brand. How are people discovered, qualified, onboarded, supported, retained, and referred? Which steps can become a simple operating system, and which must remain human?

The most useful early work is a small demand test: a sharp customer hypothesis, a simple offer, a handful of real conversations, and an honest review of conversion and delivery effort.

Good business judgement does not make a category less exciting. It stops excitement from doing the job of evidence.
